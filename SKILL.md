# Product Manager Superpower Unlock

## Purpose

At the start of a new project, most people either default to a generic playbook or wait to see what's needed before contributing. This skill does the opposite: it starts from the person's actual strengths, maps them against what the specific situation needs, and identifies the one thing that they are great at and will create the most visible value fastest before less-differentiated work fills the calendar.

The deliverable is a completed **Product Superpower Canvas** (a 2x2 grid) plus a one-line headline recommendation that names the single highest-leverage action. 

## Workflow

Work through these steps in conversation. Don't assume, but don't over-ask either. If the user has already given you enough in their message to fill a box confidently, fill it and move on; only ask about what's actually missing.

All questions in Steps 1-3 (project context, strengths, situation needs) are open-ended, not multiple-choice — ask them as plain chat messages, not via a multiple-choice question tool.

### Step 1: Gather project context

You need, at minimum:
- **Product / project name** and a one-line description of what it is
- **Start date** (or "starting now")
- **Key stakeholders** (names/roles of who this person reports to, partners with, or needs to win over)

If the user hasn't given these, ask in one message rather than one question at a time.

### Step 2: Gather the person's strengths

Strengths can arrive in several forms — adapt to whatever the user provides:
- A named framework's results (CliftonStrengths, Enneagram, etc.) — take these at face value
- Freeform input: peer feedback, manager feedback, self-reflection, performance review excerpts, or exported HR-platform data (e.g. Leapsome) if the user pastes or connects it
- Nothing — in this case, directly ask 3-4 quick surfacing questions instead of asking "what are your strengths?" directly, since most people answer that generically. Good surfacing questions:
  - What do colleagues most often come to you for?
  - What kind of work makes time disappear for you?
  - What's something you've been explicitly praised for more than once?
  - What do you do that looks effortless to you but seems to impress others?

Don't try to force strengths into a formal taxonomy unless the user already used one. Plain language ("I'm good at translating messy technical constraints into a story execs will buy") is fine and often better than a label.

### Step 3: Understand the situation's needs

Ask enough to fill in what the situation actually needs — immediate priorities, business goals, team dynamics, and what success will look like at (for example) 30/60/90 days. If the user has already described the project in detail, infer as much as you reasonably can and confirm rather than re-asking everything.

### Step 4: Populate the canvas

Fill all four quadrants using the exact structure below. Keep each box concrete and specific to this person and this project — avoid generic PM advice that could apply to anyone.

**The canvas is a one-page artifact — treat brevity as a hard constraint.** It's meant to be glanced at during a busy first week, not read like a report. 

Before writing, budget for it: 
- each quadrant gets a maximum of 3 bullets
- each bullet a single line (roughly 12-15 words — one clear idea). 
If a thought needs a "bridge" or "because" clause to land, cut it down to the core point rather than keeping the justification in the same bullet. 
When you have more good material than that, that's a sign to prioritize harder, not to shrink the font or let boxes run long — pick the 2-3 points with the most leverage and drop or merge the rest. 
After drafting, reread each quadrant and cut at least one clause or qualifier per bullet if you can.

**Quadrant 1 — What does this situation need?**
Immediate priorities, business goals, team dynamics, definition of success. This is about the situation, not the person.

**Quadrant 2 — What can I, specifically, offer here that could be valuable?**
The intersection of the person's actual strengths (from Step 2) and the situation's needs (Quadrant 1). This is the core of the exercise — don't just list strengths, map them to what THIS situation needs. If there are multiple candidate strengths, prioritize the one or two with the highest leverage (biggest gap between "needed" and "currently covered," or fastest path to visible impact).

**Quadrant 3 — How might I address any gaps I anticipate struggling to cover?**
Where the situation's needs don't map to this person's strengths. Name the gap in a few words and the bridge in a few words — e.g. "Unfamiliar with pricing mechanics → lean on revenue management as SME," not a full sentence explaining why. Be honest about gaps rather than glossing over them — this box is where credibility comes from.

**Quadrant 4 — What's the most powerful way I can harness my energy here?**
This is about finding the specific angle where addressing the situation's core needs (Quadrant 1) overlaps with what genuinely energizes and excites this person. Don't default to "communicate progress clearly" or other generic stakeholder-management advice; name the one or two moves where leaning into what excites them will also land as visibly valuable to the stakeholders named in Step 1. If Quadrant 2 identified the strength-to-need match, this box is about committing to the most energizing way to actually bring it to life.

### Step 5: Deliver the headline recommendation

Before or alongside the filled canvas, state the single highest-leverage action in **one sentence, no more than ~30 words**: "Given [strength] and [situation need], the highest-leverage move in your first two weeks is [specific action]." This sits at the top of the one-pager, so treat its length the same way as a bullet — if it needs a second sentence to make sense, the recommendation itself isn't sharp enough yet. This is what makes the exercise actionable rather than just reflective — don't bury it in the canvas alone.

### Step 6: Produce the output

If the user wants a document, fill in the canvas template — see `references/docx_rendering.md` for exact steps, a template quirk you must fix every time, and a hard 1-page requirement.

If the user just wants to talk it through without a document, that's fine too — walk through the four quadrants conversationally and still give the headline recommendation at the end, skipping the docx output.
